Low-Carb Basics: How a Low-Carb Diet Works

A low-carb diet is simpler than it sounds. You eat fewer foods that turn into sugar in the body, such as bread, rice, pasta, and sweets, and you eat more vegetables, protein, and healthy fats. That is the whole idea. The details matter, but the principle is one sentence long, and anyone can start with it today.

What low-carb means

Low-carb usually means eating between 50 and 150 grams of net carbs a day, compared with 250 grams or more on a typical diet. Net carbs are total carbohydrates minus fibre, because fibre is not digested into sugar. A stricter version, keto, goes below 50 grams, usually below 20 to 30 grams, but most people do well on a moderate low-carb plan.

The point is not to ban carbohydrates completely. Vegetables, berries, and dairy all contain carbs, and they are part of a healthy low-carb diet. The foods that are cut are the ones that deliver a lot of starch or sugar with little nutrition: bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, sugary drinks, and sweets.

How it works

When you eat carbohydrates, your body breaks them into glucose, which raises blood sugar and triggers insulin. Insulin tells the body to store energy as fat. When you eat fewer carbs, blood sugar stays steadier, insulin stays lower, and the body is more likely to burn stored fat for energy.

Many people also find that low-carb eating reduces hunger, because protein and fat are more filling than refined carbs. That is why people often eat less without counting calories. The effect is real, but it is not magic: total calories still matter, and a low-carb diet full of cream and cheese can still lead to weight gain.

What to eat

Eat plenty of non-starchy vegetables such as leafy greens, broccoli, cauliflower, courgettes, peppers, and mushrooms. Eat protein at every meal: eggs, chicken, fish, beef, pork, and tofu. Use healthy fats such as olive oil, butter, avocado, nuts, and seeds. Full-fat dairy such as cheese and Greek yoghurt is fine in moderation.

Berries are the lowest-carb fruit and can be eaten in small amounts. Drink water, tea, and coffee, and avoid sugary drinks entirely. Read labels: sugar hides in sauces, dressings, and processed foods under many names.

Getting started

Start by removing the biggest sources of carbs: sugary drinks, bread, rice, pasta, and potatoes. Replace them with vegetables, not with low-carb substitutes, because vegetables add nutrients and fibre. Plan your first week of meals in advance, and keep your kitchen stocked with low-carb staples so you are not tempted.

Expect the first week to feel strange. Some people get headaches, tiredness, or cravings as their body adjusts. Drink plenty of water, eat enough salt, and do not cut calories too hard at the same time. If you take medicine for diabetes or blood pressure, talk to your doctor before starting, because your doses may need adjusting.

A sample low-carb day

A typical low-carb day might look like this. Breakfast: scrambled eggs with spinach and feta, and a black coffee. Lunch: a chicken salad with olive oil dressing, avocado, and a handful of nuts. Dinner: salmon with roasted broccoli and butter, followed by berries with cream.

The whole day has about 40 grams of net carbs, with plenty of protein and vegetables. It is filling, it uses ordinary ingredients, and it takes less than an hour of cooking in total. Use it as a template, and swap the protein and vegetables for ones you prefer. A low-carb day does not need to be complicated to work.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many carbs should I eat on a low-carb diet?

Most people aim for 50 to 150 g of net carbs a day. Below 50 g is stricter and closer to keto. Start at the higher end and reduce gradually.

Will I feel hungry on low-carb?

Most people feel less hungry, because protein and fat are filling. If you are hungry, eat more vegetables and protein rather than reaching for carbs.

Is low-carb safe for everyone?

Most healthy adults can eat low-carb safely, but people with diabetes, kidney disease, or who are pregnant should talk to a doctor first.
