Low-Carb and Blood Sugar: What Happens When You Cut Carbs

Blood sugar is the reason many people start a low-carb diet. When you eat carbohydrates, your blood sugar rises, and your body releases insulin to bring it down. When you eat fewer carbs, blood sugar rises less, insulin stays lower, and energy stays steadier. Understanding this is the key to understanding why low-carb works, and why it needs care if you take medicine.

How carbs affect blood sugar

Carbohydrates are broken down into glucose, which enters the blood. The faster the carbs are digested, the faster blood sugar rises. Refined carbs such as white bread, rice, and sugary drinks cause a sharp spike, followed by a crash that leaves you hungry and tired.

Fibre slows digestion, which is why whole vegetables and berries have a gentler effect than refined carbs. Protein and fat have very little effect on blood sugar, which is why a low-carb meal keeps blood sugar steadier than a high-carb meal.

What low-carb does

Eating fewer carbs means smaller blood sugar rises after meals, and lower insulin levels overall. Many people with type 2 diabetes find that their blood sugar improves on a low-carb diet, and some are able to reduce their medicine, under a doctor's supervision.

Weight loss also helps blood sugar, because less body fat means the body responds better to insulin. Low-carb diets often help with both, which is why they are widely studied for type 2 diabetes.

If you take diabetes medicine

If you take insulin or other diabetes medicine, talk to your doctor before starting a low-carb diet. As your blood sugar falls, your medicine dose may need to be reduced, and if it is not, your blood sugar can fall too low, which is dangerous.

Monitor your blood sugar more often in the first weeks, and keep a record to show your doctor. Do not adjust your medicine on your own. A doctor who understands low-carb eating can help you adjust safely.

Signs to watch

A low blood sugar, or hypo, can cause shakiness, sweating, confusion, and a fast heartbeat. If you feel these symptoms and you take diabetes medicine, follow your doctor's advice for treating a hypo, which usually means taking fast-acting glucose.

If you do not take diabetes medicine, low blood sugar is unlikely on a low-carb diet. Your body will make glucose from protein and fat as needed. But if you feel unwell, eat something and talk to your doctor.

Monitoring your blood sugar

If you have diabetes, monitoring your blood sugar is part of low-carb eating. Test before meals and two hours after eating, and keep a simple log of the results, what you ate, and how you felt. Patterns will emerge: you will see which foods raise your blood sugar most, and which meals keep it steady.

Share the log with your doctor, who can use it to adjust your medicine. Do not change your medicine on your own, even if your readings improve, because the dose needs to be reduced carefully. Monitoring is a tool for you and your doctor, not a test you pass or fail.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does low-carb lower blood sugar?

Yes. Eating fewer carbs means smaller rises in blood sugar after meals and lower insulin levels overall.

I take diabetes medicine. Can I eat low-carb?

Talk to your doctor first. As blood sugar falls, your medicine dose may need reducing, or blood sugar can fall too low.

What is a hypo?

Low blood sugar, causing shakiness, sweating and confusion. It is a risk if you take insulin or some diabetes medicines.
